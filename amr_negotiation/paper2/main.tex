\begin{abstract}
A companion study benchmarked classical negotiation policies for the head-on corridor encounter, where two autonomous mobile robots meet in a single lane and one must reverse into a refuge. Here we ask whether foundation-model agents can take that role, evaluating nine large language and vision-language models as negotiators under the same physics and encounter seeds. In a centralized configuration with planner delay estimates in the prompt, reasoning-enabled models choose the right yielder in 92--93\% of 200 encounters and reach $-1.0$ to $-1.7$\,s mean excess delay relative to the best forced yielder, because they sometimes pick a better refuge than the planner default. Disabling the reasoning trace makes the same models worse than a hand-written rulebook (+11 to +30\,s versus +2.9\,s). Charging inference latency as time both robots stand still adds about 3.4\,s of excess per second of latency, so the reasoning models beat cost bidding only with sub-second answers; no measured configuration combined such latency with reasoning-level quality. Decentralized agents with egocentric views fail mainly by disagreement, in model-specific ways: one model has both robots yield on 84\% of encounters, two others have both proceed on 29--31\%. Replacing the rulebook fallback with a random tiebreak resolves every conflict but lowers right-yielder rates with egocentric images from 63--71\% to 48--63\%, whether or not the agents see the draw. Code and per-episode results are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{W}{hen} two autonomous mobile robots meet head-on in a corridor too narrow to pass, someone must reverse into a pull-in bay or out of the lane entirely. A companion benchmark~\cite{paper1} compared reactive, fixed-priority, rule-based, reservation, cost-bidding, group-bidding, Clarke-pivot, and learned negotiators under identical physics and communication models, and found that cost bidding with accurate costs tracks the best forced yielder --- a hindsight reference that tries both yield decisions --- while degrading gracefully under bid noise and misreporting. That benchmark deliberately excluded one agent family: foundation models that read a textual or visual description of the encounter and decide in natural language.

Language-model negotiators are attractive for a specific reason: the corridor decision is a small, well-posed optimization --- pick the yielder minimizing total delay --- yet it is exactly the kind of social, two-party choice that language models are claimed to handle. Warehouses are increasingly populated by robots from multiple vendors with no shared planner; an agent that can reason over a broadcast state and pick a sensible yielder without bespoke protocol engineering would be valuable. The question is whether current models can, and what it costs.

We attach a large-language-model (LLM) and vision-language-model (VLM) policy adapter to the companion benchmark and evaluate nine models (local 12--49B-parameter weights on a single Grace-Blackwell node plus a hosted 120B endpoint) across arms that isolate decision quality, inference cost, input modality, and coordination structure. All arms draw encounters from the same seed sequence, so arms run at equal size share encounters one to one. Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item a prompt-and-parse LLM/VLM negotiator for the corridor encounter, with centralized (\emph{mediator}) and decentralized (\emph{agents}) modes, text and image inputs, JSON-schema constraint, response caching, and latency charged into the simulator as decision time;
\item a model screen in which reasoning-enabled models score below the forced-yielder reference through refuge selection, and an ablation in which this advantage disappears when the reasoning trace is disabled;
\item a deployed-cost analysis: charging measured latency as frozen decision time adds about 3.4\,s of excess per second of latency, so the reasoning models' quality margin survives only for sub-second answers;
\item an egocentric decentralized study in which disagreement, not decision quality, is the dominant failure, with model-specific modes (both-yield vs.\ both-proceed);
\item a protocol-default experiment: a random tiebreak in place of the rulebook fallback resolves every conflict but lowers decision quality, because the rulebook names the right yielder on 66--71\% of conflicted encounters and the draw on 40--51\%; showing the draw to the agents changes one model's stated reasoning but not the outcome.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Language models have been applied to negotiation~\cite{marlin2024,gamechat2025}, multi-agent coordination games, and robot task planning;~\cite{garg2024llm} uses a foundation model for high-level deadlock resolution. These studies evaluate agents in bespoke environments with bespoke metrics; none compares foundation-model negotiators against a classical optimal baseline under identical physics, which the companion benchmark~\cite{paper1} provides. Vision-language models are increasingly used for robot perception and instruction following, but we are not aware of a controlled comparison of text, image, and egocentric-image inputs for an inter-robot \emph{decision} task. Closest in structure, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024scalable} compare centralized and decentralized LLM planners for multi-robot collaboration, the axis our mediator and agents modes span.

The classical alternative is market-based coordination, which allocates tasks and resources by bidding~\cite{dias2006market,gerkey2002sold}; multi-agent path finding has also been cast as a combinatorial auction~\cite{amir2015mapfauction}. The cost-bidding baseline we compare against belongs to this family. Negotiated path planning for non-cooperative robots~\cite{gautier2022negotiated} and opinion-driven decision making for narrow-corridor navigation~\cite{alghamdi2025opinion} address related conflicts without language models.

Our tiebreak arms connect to game theory: public randomization resolves anti-coordination games as a correlated equilibrium~\cite{aumann1974subjectivity}, and in bargaining theory the disagreement point shapes negotiated outcomes~\cite{nash1950bargaining}. We test whether stating the default changes what language-model agents choose. Coin flipping between mutually distrusting parties requires a commitment protocol~\cite{blum1983coin}; our main draw is a simpler publicly computable hash that assumes non-adversarial robots, and we also run a commit-reveal variant.

\section{Benchmark and Agents}
\label{sec:bench}
\subsection{The corridor encounter}
We use the companion benchmark~\cite{paper1} unchanged. Two TurtleBot3-Waffle-class unicycle robots (0.26\,m/s forward, 0.15\,m/s reverse, radius 0.15\,m) approach head-on in an 8\,m, 0.65\,m-wide single-lane corridor with a pull-in bay and end-room refuges. On conflict detection each robot broadcasts a frozen \emph{snapshot}: position, heading, speed, goal, corridor entry time, urgency, and payload state. A policy receives the two snapshots and returns a \emph{yielder}, a \emph{proceeder}, and a refuge; the yielder reverses into or toward the refuge, waits, then continues. The 2D unicycle simulator runs Monte Carlo encounters; we use the single-bay and two-bays layouts with 100 encounters per layout (200 total) from seeds 100000--100099. These seeds are disjoint from those used to train and evaluate the learned negotiator in~\cite{paper1}. Arms run at 25 encounters per layout use seeds 100000--100024, a strict subset.

An episode succeeds if both robots reach their goals without collision within the 180\,s simulation horizon; if either robot has not arrived by then, the episode is a \emph{timeout} (labeled \texttt{deadlock} in the released data). Metrics are success, collision, timeout, urgency-weighted excess delay against the best forced yielder, and \emph{right-yielder rate}. The forced-yielder reference replays both yield assignments, each with the planner's default refuge, and keeps the better. Right-yielder rate is the share of encounters whose first decision names the reference's yielder, excluding encounters where the two forced outcomes differ by less than 0.5\,s. Excess is averaged over successful episodes; success is reported separately. Whenever the LLM answer is unusable (server error, parse failure, a yielder with no feasible refuge, or disagreement in agents mode), the companion benchmark's default rulebook decides, and the fallback fraction reports how often. The fallback decision takes effect only after the charged decision time (Sec.~\ref{sec:adapter}) has elapsed. Under measured latency a slow failed call can therefore push an episode past the horizon even though the rulebook supplies a decision. Every zero-cost run in this paper succeeds.

\subsection{LLM policy adapter}
\label{sec:adapter}
The \texttt{llm} policy issues OpenAI-compatible chat requests at decision time with temperature 0, seed 0, and a 4096-token output cap. In \emph{mediator} mode one call describes both snapshots and asks for the yielder. In \emph{agents} mode each robot is prompted separately (``You are robot $X$'') and answers yield or proceed; exactly one yield and one proceed are required, and any other combination falls back. Two information levels exist. \emph{Raw} gives the natural-language state: positions, headings, speeds, urgency, load, and feasible refuges with distances. \emph{Plans} adds, for each refuge, both robots' delays predicted by the kinematic planner, the same numbers cost bidding uses. The rulebook does not see these numbers when choosing the yielder; it uses the planner only to pick the yielder's refuge. Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} gives the prompts verbatim.

Responses are constrained by a JSON schema with three required fields: a free-text \texttt{reason} of at most 300 characters; the decision (\texttt{yielder}, enumerated over the two robot IDs, or \texttt{action}, enumerated over yield and proceed); and a \texttt{refuge}, enumerated over the layout's refuge IDs plus \texttt{any}. A named refuge that is infeasible for the yielder is replaced by the planner's choice.

Replies are cached on a SHA-256 hash of the full request. Within a run, identical prompts therefore return identical answers, and an interrupted run resumes exactly; a replayed call is charged its original latency but takes no wall time. Caching does not make the model deterministic across runs: each run starts a fresh cache, and batched GPU inference is not bitwise reproducible at temperature 0. Rerunning one configuration in separate runs moved nemotron-omni's egocentric-agent disagreement count between 167 and 173 of 200, and thinking-off qwen3.8-27B mediator+plans excess from 11.1 to 14.9\,s. We treat differences of this size as run-to-run variation.

Two cost modes isolate quality from deployment. \texttt{think=0} charges no decision time and measures decision quality alone. \texttt{think=measured} charges the real response latency: each robot evaluates the prompts itself and brakes until the answer arrives. The second robot's identical request hits the cache and is charged the original latency, so both robots stand still for the same time. In agents mode the two per-robot prompts would run on separate robots in parallel, so the charge is the slower of the two calls. Measured latency depends on how requests reach the server. Runs with four concurrent episodes share one vLLM server, and the server log of the concurrent qwen3.8-27B run shows four sequences decoding at once; their latency therefore includes contention for the GPU. We label each measured arm \emph{serial} (one episode at a time) or \emph{concurrent} (four).

\subsection{Vision arms}
Two renderers feed VLMs the encounter as an image. \emph{Global}: a top-down map showing walls, refuge parking marks, both robots as labeled discs with heading arrows, urgency/load annotations, and goal stars. \emph{Egocentric}: the same render clipped to each robot's forward view ($-1.5$ to $+6.5$\,m along its heading) --- the agents mode's onboard-camera analogue, so each robot sees only what it would sense, while the broadcast text still carries the peer's state. Modality arms compare text-only, image-only, and text+image on the global view, and text vs.\ egocentric image on the decentralized mode.

\subsection{Models}
We screen nine models (Table~\ref{tab:models}): eight served locally on one NVIDIA GB10 node (vLLM, NVFP4 quantization, per-family reasoning parsers) plus hosted \texttt{nemotron-3-super-120b}. A tenth local model, Muse-Glimmer-30B, failed to load because the serving stack does not support its architecture. Reasoning models emit a trace before answering; a per-request switch (\texttt{enable\_thinking=false}) disables it where supported. \emph{Thinking-off} means the model emits the decision directly, and we verified that the recorded reasoning field is empty in every thinking-off episode. \emph{Thinking-on} means it writes a trace first.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
The screen runs mediator+plans with \texttt{think=0} and each model's default reasoning setting on 200 encounters. The screen rows predate trace logging; reruns of the same configuration on 50 encounters (\texttt{on0}) recorded a trace in every episode. The top three screen models then enter a matrix over mode, information level, thinking, and cost mode. Thinking-off cells run 200 encounters; thinking-on cells beyond the screen run 50, because their traces made them several times slower. A 50-encounter arm is paired with a 200-encounter arm only on the 50 shared encounters. On that subset the baselines differ (rulebook 3.7\,s, fixed priority 10.3\,s, versus 2.9 and 10.7\,s on 200), and we report each arm against baselines on its own encounters. Vision, egocentric, and tiebreak arms run thinking-off, \texttt{think=0}, 200 encounters. Table~\ref{tab:results} lists $n$, excess with 95\% CI, right-yielder rate, and fallback composition for every arm we report. \subsection{Model screen}
Figure~\ref{fig:screen} and Table~\ref{tab:results} screen all models on the mediator+plans arm (\texttt{think=0}, 200 encounters). Reasoning-enabled mixture-of-experts models cluster tightly: qwen3.8-27B, qwen3-30B, and hosted nemotron-3-super reach $-1.7$\,s mean excess, nemotron-nano $-1.5$\,s, and nemotron-omni $-1.0$\,s, with right-yielder rates of 92--93\%. Excess below the reference is possible because the reference fixes each yielder's refuge to the planner's choice, which minimizes \emph{unweighted} predicted delay, whereas the prompt asks for urgency-weighted delay and the models sometimes name a different refuge. Cost bidding, which uses the same predicted delays, matches the reference (0.0\,s, 99\%). Nemotron-lightning reaches 1.7\,s (80\%), but 66\% of its encounters fall back on parse failures because its traces exhaust the 4096-token cap, so its excess is largely the rulebook's. The remaining models score worse than the rulebook (2.9\,s, 72\%): gemma4-26B at 6.1\,s (75\%), nemotron-super-49B at 15.8\,s (42\%), and nemotron-vl-12B at 21.7\,s (38\%), the last two beyond fixed priority (10.7\,s). % VERIFIED 2026-10-23: screen caches for super-49b and vl-12b carry the reasoning key (all empty); gemma4's screen predates logging but its post-logging arms (ego/nonce/silent/vlm, 3400 calls) have empty reasoning on every call, as do vl-12b's (2200 calls). Reasoning-enabled models log nonempty traces on 100% of calls, so absence is real: all three are non-reasoning.
A 1.5B model on a different serving stack (llama.cpp, 4-bit GGUF; 20 encounters, \texttt{think=0}) reached 28.0--30.5\,s and 25--30\% right in mediator mode; these runs used a 256-token output cap instead of 4096.

\subsection{The deployed cost}
\label{sec:latency}
Under \texttt{think=measured} both robots stand still until the answer arrives (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}, Table~\ref{tab:results}). Serial thinking-on runs give the deployed cost on the 50-encounter subset. For qwen3-30B mediator+plans, the mean charged latency is 16.5\,s, and excess rises from $-1.3$\,s at zero cost on the same encounters to $+55.3$\,s, while the right-yielder rate is 92\%, against 94\% at zero cost. For qwen3.8-27B the latency is 49.9\,s, excess $+157.9$\,s over 49 successful episodes, and right-yielder 94\%. Its one timeout follows a 119.5\,s call: the charged wait alone uses two thirds of the 180\,s horizon and leaves less than 60\,s for the yield maneuver and both robots' remaining travel. That answer also named the wrong yielder.

The slope follows from the cost model rather than a fit: two robots idling at the mean urgency weight of 5/3 accrue about 3.3\,s of weighted delay per second of latency. Every measured arm with resolvable latency corroborates it. The serial thinking-on arms give $(55.3+1.3)/16.5\approx3.4$\,s/s for qwen3-30B and $(157.9+1.3)/49.9\approx3.2$\,s/s for qwen3.8-27B. Serial thinking-off qwen3.8-27B goes from 11.1 to 34.1\,s at 6.7\,s latency, also 3.4\,s/s. The serial thinking-off qwen3-30B arm (0.7\,s) is too fast to resolve the slope against its $\pm$3.5\,s CI.

Concurrent execution inflates latency. Thinking-on qwen3-30B charged 31.1\,s concurrently against 16.5\,s serially, and with thinking off 22.0\,s against 0.7\,s. The concurrent thinking-on arm (103.1\,s excess) is therefore an upper bound on what a single deployed mediator would pay, although its 104.4\,s increase over 31.1\,s also gives 3.4\,s/s. The decentralized measured arms in the concurrent runs fare worse (thinking-on qwen3-30B agents+plans: 172.9\,s excess at 51.9\,s latency) and are in the released data. A serial thinking-on run of the hosted nemotron-3-super completed, but every call returned an error within 0.1\,s, so it measures error latency, not deliberation.

The qwen3.8-27B serial thinking-on arm has incomplete provenance. Its output directory was first used by a run with the same model, settings, and one episode worker that also covered other arms and was stopped after at least 10 of the 50 encounters. The final run, restricted to mediator+plans, replayed that run's cached answers. Its summed charged latency (about 2495\,s) exceeds its wall time (1530\,s) by more than the 572\,s of latency in the ten encounters the log shows complete. Either the stopped run completed at least 18 encounters, which its log neither confirms nor excludes, or the final run used more than one episode worker, which its metadata does not record. In the second case the 49.9\,s includes contention and overstates the deployed latency. The serial qwen3-30B arm has no such gap (839\,s wall time against 825\,s summed latency), and its 16.5\,s alone exceeds the break-even latency below by an order of magnitude, so the conclusion does not rest on the qwen3.8-27B value. % 2026-10-24: qwen3.8-27b-onm-s provenance checked against stage2c.log, stage2c_measured.sh, meta.json and rows.jsonl; cache.jsonl not retained, final invocation not logged. Cumulative think_time of single_bay seeds 100000-100016 = 906 s, -100017 = 981 s; ~965-979 s needed.

On raw prompts, thinking-on with measured latency succeeds in only 64\% of 50 encounters. There, 88\% of decisions are parse failures after traces hit the token cap, the mean charged latency is 100.3\,s, and the rulebook decision that follows arrives too late for both robots to finish within the 180\,s horizon. The 301.5\,s excess over the successful 32 episodes excludes the slowest cases, so we do not compare it with other arms.

The slope gives a break-even estimate. On the 200-encounter screen, a reasoning mediator has a quality margin of 1.0--1.7\,s against cost bidding, which survives only if mean latency stays below about 0.3--0.5\,s. Against the rulebook (2.9\,s) the margin is 3.9--4.6\,s, and the latency limit is about 1.1--1.4\,s. Thinking-off models reach sub-second latency (0.7\,s for qwen3-30B) but lose the margin itself (23.6\,s excess). No measured configuration combined sub-second latency with reasoning-level quality. At current inference speeds, cost bidding, which computes its bids from the planner without model inference, dominates; an LLM negotiator becomes viable only if deliberation leaves the critical path through precomputation, asynchronous negotiation, or much faster inference.

\subsection{Egocentric decentralized agents}
\label{sec:ego}
Chance encounters between unaffiliated robots motivate the agents mode: each robot gets only its egocentric view plus the peer's broadcast and decides independently. All arms here run thinking off, \texttt{think=0}, 200 encounters. Disagreement, not decision quality, dominates, and its form differs by model (Fig.~\ref{fig:disagree}). Nemotron-omni has both robots volunteer to yield on 84\% of encounters (167 of 200) and never has both proceed on this arm; the rulebook then decides. Qwen3.8-27B and vl-12b have both robots proceed on 29\% and 31\%; each robot's reason cites proximity facts that assign the yield to the other robot. Gemma4 agrees on 55\%, and 85 of its 91 conflicts are both-yield. Agreement does not imply a good choice. Excluding ties, the right-yielder rate on agreed encounters is 58\% for omni (19 of 33), 69\% for qwen3.8 (77 of 112), 64\% for vl-12b (59 of 92), and 71\% for gemma4 (77 of 108). Wrong-yielder agreements (14, 35, 33, and 31 encounters) execute without any fallback, at mean excesses of 13.9, 39.1, 17.8, and 14.1\,s respectively. % VERIFIED 2026-10-23: *-ego rows, label llm_agents_img, rule==llm_agents, yielder != best_yielder != tie
Ego images give lower excess than raw text for vl-12b (5.3 vs 7.4\,s) and qwen3.8 (8.9 vs 10.0\,s) but higher for omni (3.4 vs 3.0\,s) and gemma4 (3.8 vs 2.8\,s). Omni's raw-text arm falls back on 97\% of encounters, so its excess is essentially the rulebook's. Planner delays help only gemma4 agree: with plans it disagrees on 21\% of encounters and reaches 88\% right-yielder at 3.1\,s, whereas qwen3.8, omni, and vl-12b disagree on 137, 199, and 194 of 200, more than with egocentric images (87, 167, and 103). \subsection{The protocol default}
\label{sec:tiebreak}
Disagreement calls for a tiebreak. We test a random draw in place of the rulebook fallback. Each robot's card is the SHA-256 hash of its ID, both IDs, and the detection time in the snapshot of robot A (the lower ID), which both robots use; both compute the card from broadcast data, neither chooses its value, and the larger card proceeds on conflict. A robot could steer its card by shifting the detection time it broadcasts, so the plain draw is unsteerable only if robots report honestly. Because the IDs are the same in every encounter, the card pair depends on the detection time alone, and detection times repeat across encounters: the 200 encounters produce only 92 distinct card pairs. Encounters that share a detection time share their draw, so the draws are not independent coins. Robot A holds the larger card in 120 of the 200 encounters (60\%)---2.8 standard deviations from an even split if the draws were independent---and in 54 of the 92 distinct pairs (59\%), 1.7 standard deviations from even over the effective sample; the imbalance is real but not confined to repeated keys.

We also implemented a commit-reveal variant~\cite{blum1983coin} (\texttt{tiebreak=commit}), in which each robot commits to a nonce before the peer's reveal and the card material is the two nonces alone, so nothing a robot chooses to broadcast can steer its card. In simulation the nonces derive from the encounter seed, which the two layouts share, so the commit draw has 100 distinct card pairs, each used twice; robot A holds the larger card in 55 of them (110 of 200 encounters). We ran the commit draw on a 200-encounter run under nemotron-lightning in agents mode with raw text and no image, because this model has no vision input. It resolves every conflict without fallback ($12.4\pm3.0$\,s and 58\% right with the draw visible, $13.1\pm3.2$\,s and 55\% hidden), against $4.8\pm1.8$\,s, 71\% right, and 80\% fallback for the rulebook default in the same run. As with the plain draw below, the random draw is a worse fallback than the informed rulebook. Its model and input level differ from the egocentric arms, so it is not in Table~\ref{tab:tiebreak}. % VERIFIED 2026-10-24: commit-reveal implemented in llm.py (_commit_draws), run end-to-end (results_raw/llm/nemotron-lightning-commit). Plain draw replayed headless with a snapshot-time probe (200 encounters): 92 distinct keys, A proceeds in 120 encounters and in 54 of the 92 distinct pairs.

Three plain-draw arms, all egocentric agents with thinking off, separate presentation from resolution (Table~\ref{tab:tiebreak}, Fig.~\ref{fig:tiebreak}): the rulebook default; a hidden draw, whose prompts are byte-identical to the rulebook arm; and a visible draw, whose prompt states the rule and both card values (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

The hidden draw isolates resolution. Its prompts match the rulebook arm, so within a run its answers are the same cached responses (omni: 170 both-yield, 27 agreed, 3 both-proceed in both arms), and only the resolution of conflicts changes. On the conflicted encounters the rulebook names the right yielder 66--71\% of the time and the draw 40--51\%: omni 71 vs 47\%, qwen3.8 67 vs 51\%, gemma4 70 vs 51\%, and vl-12b 66 vs 40\%. Arm-level excess rises from 3.1--10.6\,s to 7.8--14.0\,s, and right-yielder rates fall from 63--71\% to 49--63\%.

The visible draw adds presentation. The draw still resolves every conflict, so no image-arm encounter falls back to the rulebook (vl-12b's text-only arm has 10 parse fallbacks), but conflicting answers persist. Both robots yield or both proceed on 126 (omni), 83 (qwen3.8), 102 (gemma4), and 104 (vl-12b) of 200 encounters, against 169, 80, 84, and 97 under the rulebook default in the same runs. Only omni engages with the draw at scale. At least one omni robot cites the card or draw on 183 of 200 encounters (``A's card is smaller than B's, so if both yield or proceed, B would proceed''), against 28, 27, and 2 for the other three models, and only omni's conflict count falls. Outcomes match the hidden draw: right-yielder 48--59\% and excess 7.6--15.1\,s with egocentric images, and 45--56\% and 8.2--16.6\,s with text only.

A random draw is therefore a poor default here: it resolves every conflict but discards the information the rulebook uses on exactly the encounters where agents fail to agree. Showing the draw changed what one model wrote, not how often the right robot yielded.

\section{Discussion}
Decision quality and deployment cost point in different directions. Where a shared decision service is acceptable, a reasoning mediator with planner delays already scores at or below the forced-yielder reference at zero decision cost. The engineering problem is to take deliberation off the critical path, for example by negotiating predicted encounters ahead of time, caching likely decisions, or routing routine cases to a fast tier. For chance encounters between unaffiliated robots, decentralized foundation-model agents are not yet deployable: in the egocentric arm, conflicting answers occur on 44--84\% of encounters, depending on model, and the fallback then decides. The fallback therefore matters. An informed rulebook recovers 66--71\% right-yielder decisions on those encounters, whereas the random draw recovers 40--51\%, whether or not the agents see it.

\subsection{Limitations}
All results are from simulation; the Gazebo transfer validated in~\cite{paper1} covers classical policies, not the LLM arms. The benchmark, baselines, and reference come from the companion submission~\cite{paper1}, which is under review; Sec.~\ref{sec:bench} restates the elements this paper relies on. Two layouts and one encounter type bound the generality. Serial thinking-on latency is measured for two models on 50 encounters each, and one of the two runs was resumed from a stopped run with an unrecorded worker count (Sec.~\ref{sec:latency}). Thinking-on cells beyond the screen use 50 encounters. All local models ran with NVFP4 quantization; absolute decision quality may differ at full precision. Runs of identical configurations vary (Sec.~\ref{sec:adapter}), and we report one run per arm. The hosted endpoint failed on 58--100\% of calls outside the screen. Thinking-off is honored on the models tested but is not universal across serving stacks, and several models' traces exceed the 4096-token cap on raw prompts, as the parse-fallback counts show. Agents answer a single-shot prompt; multi-turn dialogue, tool use (querying the planner), and selfish-utility prompts are untested.

\section{Conclusion}
Reasoning-enabled language models can negotiate head-on robot encounters at or below the forced-yielder reference when decision time is free, and the reasoning trace, not the model weights alone, carries that ability: with the trace disabled, the same models fall below a hand-written rulebook. Deployed viability depends on latency. Each second of latency costs about 3.4\,s of excess, so at measured inference speeds cost bidding remains the better mechanism. For decentralized agents, the bottleneck is coordination, not perception: models disagree in model-specific ways that richer inputs do not remove for all of them. When agents disagree, the protocol's fallback decides the outcome, and an informed fallback outperforms a random draw.
