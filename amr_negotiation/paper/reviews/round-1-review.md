VERDICT: MAJOR

SUMMARY
This letter presents a reproducible benchmark for head-on two-robot encounters in a single-lane corridor with pull-in bays, running identical agent code in a 2D unicycle simulator and in ROS 2/Gazebo with TurtleBot3 Waffles. It compares eight policy families (reactive, fixed priority, rulebook, reservation, cost bidding, group bidding, Clarke pivot, and a tabular learned baseline) against a "best forced yielder" hindsight reference, across bay geometry, asymmetry, bid noise, strategic misreporting, repeated encounters, packet loss/latency, convoys, a T-junction, inconsistent maps, blocked bays, and explore mode. The draft is unusually candid about its own limits (layout-dependent reservation result, small map-study samples, non-independent T-junction validation), and its arithmetic checks out almost everywhere I could verify it (460 = 122+338 = sum of Table II rows; 13 first-yielder disagreements = 5+6+1+1; Wilson bounds 1.1%/360 and 0.85%/450 are correct; the Clarke pivot formula and its DSIC direction are correct for cost reports; the 5,760-episode ablation = 2^3 arms x 2 policies x 360). The concerns below are about what the central claims rest on, not about sloppiness.

MAJOR CONCERNS

1. **The headline "cost bidding matches the reference" is near-tautological under the stated setup, and the abstract/conclusion do not carry the caveat.** Sec. IV-A (line 72) admits "the bids use the simulator's own kinematics," and the reference is computed by running forced outcomes in the same deterministic simulator. With deterministic dynamics and noiseless bids, predicted delay ~= realized delay, so Delta~=0 follows largely by construction -- the experiment validates the implementation more than it establishes a policy advantage. The informative result is the robustness degradation (Fig. 2a) and the asymmetry study. Resolution: (a) report prediction-vs-realized delay error explicitly (this quantifies how circular the match is); (b) add a *structural* model-mismatch test -- the log-normal scalar noise on bids does not probe wrong speed limits, wrong refuge distances, or wrong free-flow estimates, which are the realistic failure modes -- or narrow the abstract/conclusion claim to "matches the reference when bids equal the simulator's own cost model." Related: the Clarke result "no unilateral misreport pays off" (line 89) is guaranteed by the VCG theorem under the shared cost model; 10,080 episodes confirm implementation, which should be framed as such, and the deviation space tested (scalar k on reports) ignores richer manipulations available in your own protocol -- e.g., a robot omitting known bays from its BID changes the feasible-outcome support of r_i(o), which is a manipulation your map studies show matters. At minimum, state that refuge-set manipulation is untested.

2. **Reproducibility of the benchmark -- the paper's first contribution -- is under-specified.** For a benchmark letter, the artifact and its configuration space are the contribution, yet: (a) no repository link, artifact DOI, or even an anonymized URL appears anywhere despite "Code and per-episode results are released" (abstract, conclusion); (b) the episode time limit is never given anywhere in the manuscript (it is used in the success/deadlock definitions, Sec. III-D); (c) the reactive policy's "random patience time" distribution, the commit timeout value (only the 15 s hold_for_map timeout is given), acceleration limits, controller/integration details, the latency/jitter distributions, and how collision geometry relates to the "0.25 m swept radius" vs. "overlap of footprint rectangles" are all absent. A single parameter/configuration table plus an artifact citation would resolve this; without it, "benchmark" is aspirational.

3. **Study-design accounting does not let the reader reconstruct the experimental matrix.** Episode counts appear scattered and do not decompose transparently: "2,940 single-bay episodes" is 7 policies x 420, but which 7 is never stated (does it include group_bid? the rulebook variant? forced-yielder runs?); the asymmetry study's 5,040 episodes have no stated factorization; the 10,080 Clarke episodes only decompose if one assumes ~28 cells per config (10 k x A/B/joint with duplicates removed) x 6 configs x 60 draws -- readers shouldn't have to guess; the "1,440 episodes" under extreme loss vs. the 5,760-episode factorial ablation vs. the 3,600/4,320-episode milder grids overlap unclearly. Also, "delays are averaged over successful episodes only" (line 66) censors the reactive baseline's worst 6.2% of episodes, so the 97 s / 3.6x figures are optimistic lower bounds on reactive cost -- this is directionally fine but should be stated explicitly next to the number. Resolution: one table listing, per study: scenario, grid factors, draws/cell, policies run, N, loss/latency levels.

4. **The largest 2D<->Gazebo discrepancy -- the T-junction -- is unexplained on the final code.** Sec. IV-H reports Delta_D = -16.8 s mean, cost_bid finishing 22 s earlier in Gazebo, and second yielders in 7/36 runs that never occur in 2D; the only causal account ("renegotiation stall in 2D") comes from a trace on *earlier* code, and "we have not re-traced these runs" (line 137). Contribution 4 and the abstract's "conclusions transfer" framing rest on this being understood. Either re-trace the final-code runs, or explicitly scope the transfer claim to the scenarios where it is demonstrated. The authors' own disclosure that the T-junction rows double as bug-finding cases (not independent validation) makes leaving this unexplained more conspicuous, not less.

5. **Baseline strength: the only non-communicating baseline is deliberately naive, and the motivating failure mode is never demonstrated.** The reactive policy (random patience, back to nearest refuge, both robots back off in 39% of episodes) is close to a strawman; a deterministic no-communication social law (e.g., yield-on-ID-priority inferable from a sensor signature, or right-hand-rule refuge choice) would test whether *messages* or merely *coordination* drives the 93.8%->100% gap. Separately, the introduction motivates the problem by reciprocal-avoidance/CBF deadlock, citing prior work, but no ORCA/RVO/CBF condition is ever run in this benchmark -- so the premise is asserted, not shown, in the authors' own physics. I am not demanding a full controller integration; either run one reciprocal-avoidance condition or state plainly that the deadlock premise is taken from the cited literature and was not reproduced here.

MINOR COMMENTS

- **Abstract (line 14):** ~350 words, well over RA-L's abstract limit; must be cut roughly in half. Also "the negotiating policies succeed in every episode" is scoped to the single-bay study only by grammar; elsewhere in the paper negotiating policies do fail (T-junction pre-fix, blocked bay 10/10) -- scope it explicitly. "In 2,940 single-bay episodes the reactive baseline succeeds in 93.8%" reads as if n=2,940 for reactive; it is 420.
- **Line 87:** "an earlier payment rule we implemented" is a phantom antecedent -- the rule is introduced mid-sentence with no prior referent; either list it among the evaluated policies in Sec. III-C or delete the episode-level numbers (A gained 7.5 s, B 0.7 s) as anecdote.
- **Lines 94, 133, 137:** "an earlier version of the protocol," "the merged main branch," "older code" -- development-history language that sits awkwardly against contribution 2's "all on one code version." Clarify: the 2D numbers are one version; Gazebo is another (final) version; earlier-version results are reported only as motivation for fixes.
- **Table II / line 135:** "437 of 450 chose the same first yielder" includes the 44 forced-yielder rows where agreement is trivial by construction. Report agreement on negotiating-policy runs (393/406) -- still a strong result, and more honest.
- **Line 89:** "utility, its delay plus payment" silently assumes quasi-linearity -- 1 s of delay credit = 1 s of delay. State this assumption and whether credit is fungible across encounters (which couples it to the Sec. IV-B fairness study; a robot hoarding credit could change the truthfulness analysis across repeated encounters).
- **Line 122:** map studies at 10 seeds/cell are appropriately flagged, but several convoy (72/cell) and T-junction (48/cell) numbers are reported as bare means -- add CIs, at least where a claim rests on a difference (e.g., 34.4 vs 33.7 s).
- **Lines 96-97 / Table I:** the text's "98.9-99.2% per policy" for the default protocol under extreme loss should be reconciled with cost_bid's 99.4-99.7% rows in the table -- the stated range seems to describe only the all-safeguards-on arms; say so.
- **Line 40:** "0.65 m corridor" vs. two Waffles -- if collision is rectangle overlap (~0.61 m side-by-side < 0.65 m), "two cannot pass" needs the clearance/footprint rule that makes passing infeasible stated in one sentence.
- **Line 40:** B's start delay U[0,8] s -- state that A's traversal time guarantees a head-on encounter for every draw (otherwise some cells may contain trivially non-conflicting episodes).
- **Line 74:** the reservation result is honestly flagged as layout-dependent; consider one sentence noting that the 210-encounter reservation grid (30 draws/cell) differs from the 60-draw main grid, since a reader will otherwise assume same-draw pairing.
- **Line 54:** "Reservation" spec is under-determined -- "first come" = corridor entry time? What does a robot do when both are outside? One sentence.
- **Line 130:** "an unseen single-bay layout" -- unseen in what respect (new bay positions? different corridor length)? The generalization claim depends on this.
- **Line 91:** Jain's index is applied to cumulative *delays* (a cost, where the usual convention assumes a good); state the convention or the transformation.
- **Line 96:** "In 14-24% of episodes both robots started a yield before one gave way" -- worth one sentence on why the yield/pass logic guarantees termination of these mutual-yield episodes (no livelock bound is given anywhere).
- **Fig. 1 caption:** "(d) robot S starts in the side branch" -- but Sec. IV-E also describes a turning-into-branch variant; the caption should note which variant is drawn, or that S's start pose swaps by condition.
- **Line 22:** "no study compares the families under the same physics and communication model" -- strong scope claim; defensible, but consider "to our knowledge" given the breadth of AGV zone-control literature you cite (e.g., geozone2023, fanti2002event are in refs.bib but never cited in text -- either cite or prune; several other .bib entries appear unused for this letter).

QUESTIONS FOR THE AUTHORS

1. What is the episode time limit in each study, and what does the reactive baseline's mean delay (and the 3.6x ratio) become if deadlocked episodes are counted at the time limit rather than censored?
2. Which seven policies (and how many forced-yielder runs) make up the 2,940 single-bay episodes, and how do the 5,040 / 10,080 / 1,440 / 3,600 / 4,320 episode counts factor? A design table answering this at a glance would materially strengthen the "benchmark" contribution.
3. Under what clearance/footprint model is passing infeasible in the 0.65 m corridor?
4. In the Clarke study, can a robot manipulate by misreporting its *known refuge set* (not just scaling its bid), and is the commit step's lower-ID tie-break exploitable by a strategic agent?
5. Was any reciprocal-avoidance-only controller (ORCA/RVO/CBF) ever run in this corridor? If it deadlocks as the introduction predicts, including it would close the motivational loop at low cost; if it doesn't, the premise needs revising.
6. Where will the code, scenarios, study scripts, and per-episode results be hosted (anonymized link acceptable for review)?
7. Is the "same first yielder" agreement of 437/450 computed including forced-yielder runs; what is it on negotiating policies alone?
8. The 0.5 m/s robots in the 1.5 m-detection comms study vs. the Waffle's 0.26 m/s in Gazebo -- acknowledged, but does any 2D comms conclusion change qualitatively at 0.26 m/s?
