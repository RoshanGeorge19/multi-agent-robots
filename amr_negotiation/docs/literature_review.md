## 1. Executive summary

The planned study puts two (later three or more) AMRs head-on in a corridor that is only one robot wide and has one or more pull-in bays (passing places). One robot has to reverse or detour into a bay so the other can pass. The literature that bears on this problem is large, but it is split across at least nine communities that rarely cite one another:

1. **Deadlock theory for reactive controllers.** Control-barrier-function (CBF) and velocity-obstacle controllers stall in symmetric, constrained configurations [@grover2023before; @grover2020deadlock; @zheng2023deadlock; @dergachev2021distributed]. This failure motivates the "no communication" baseline.
2. **Fleet coordination with formal guarantees.** Loosely coupled supervisory controllers and robust plan execution give safety and liveness guarantees under delays and communication faults [@cap2016provably; @pecora2018loosely; @mannucci2019provably; @mannucci2021provably; @honig2019persistent].
3. **AGV zone control and industrial traffic management**, from Petri-net and zone-based deadlock avoidance to hierarchical traffic managers [@fanti2002event; @reveliotis2000conflict; @geozone2023; @hiertraffic2023], and the interfaces that industry standardised: Open-RMF/`rmf_traffic`, VDA 5050, MassRobotics and ISO 3691-4 [@openrmf; @rmftraffic; @vda5050; @massrobotics; @iso36914].
4. **MAPF and prioritised planning**, the natural centralised oracle [@sharon2015cbs; @barer2014suboptimal; @okumura2022pibt; @okumura2023lacam; @li2021lifelong]. This includes recent narrow-corridor work [@experience2024narrow; @nodetorobot2023; @macga2025] and kinodynamic work [@kottinger2022conflict; @wen2022clmapf; @dbecbs2025].
5. **Explicit negotiation, auctions and mechanism design**, from Contract Net to non-cooperative MAPF auctions [@smith1980contract; @gerkey2002sold; @dias2006market; @gautier2022negotiated; @amir2015mapfauction].
6. **Game-theoretic "social mini-games"**: doorways, hallways and intersections treated as non-cooperative games with CBF-based liveness [@chandra2025deadlock; @chandra2026social; @chandra2023decentralized].
7. **Multi-agent RL**, including give-way/corridor scenarios in VMAS [@bettini2022vmas; @bettini2023heterogeneous; @vmasgiveway] and learned hallway passing [@park2023hallucination].
8. **LLM-based negotiation (2023 onward)**: RoCo [@mandi2024roco], foundation-model deadlock resolution [@garg2024llm], GameChat [@gamechat2025] and MARLIN [@marlin2024]. MARLIN uses **two TurtleBot3 Waffle robots in slotted corridors**, which makes it the closest existing physical setup to this study.
9. **Road-traffic analogues**: single-track roads with passing places [@highwaycode; @narrowpassageuk2023], bottleneck negotiation between connected vehicles [@bottleneckiv2023; @avmvbottleneck2023] and auction/game-theoretic intersection management [@dresner2008multiagent; @carlino2013auction].

**Main finding.** No widely adopted benchmark exists for *robot-robot* head-on corridor negotiation with refuges or bays. Nobody has compared the families above (reactive, fixed priority, rulebook, bidding, centralised oracle, LLM, MARL) on one physics-based ROS 2 platform with common metrics. The nearest scenario assets are the VMAS `give_way` scenario (a corridor with one asymmetric opening) [@vmasgiveway], MARLIN's "single-slot" and "two-slot" corridors [@marlin2024], the doorway, hallway and intersection "social mini-games" [@chandra2026social], and width-1 corridors in grid MAPF [@experience2024narrow]. None of these combines continuous nonholonomic reversing, choice of bay, asymmetric urgency or cost, communication impairment, and repeated-encounter fairness. That combination is the opening for this project (Section 6).

## 2. Scope, method and key distinctions

**Search and verification.** Candidate papers were collected from the topics in the brief, from the reference lists of the recent surveys [@chandra2026social; @mirsky2024conflict; @francis2025principles; @fragapane2021planning; @stern2019mapf], and by forward search on Crossref and OpenAlex. Each candidate was confirmed by DOI resolution, an exact-title match in Crossref or OpenAlex, an arXiv ID lookup, or by fetching its stable URL. Remembered references that could not be confirmed were dropped. About 20 works were read in full or in large part (Section 4). The rest are summarised from abstracts and standard knowledge of the field.

The same words mean different things in different communities, so the review uses five distinctions throughout:

| Distinction | Meaning in this review | Why it matters for the pull-in-bay study |
|---|---|---|
| **Collision avoidance vs. deadlock resolution** | Collision avoidance keeps robots apart (safety). Deadlock or livelock resolution makes sure they eventually reach their goals (liveness). | Safe reactive controllers such as ORCA, CBFs and the ROS 2 Nav2 local planners can be perfectly safe and still deadlock in a one-lane corridor [@grover2023before; @dergachev2021distributed]. |
| **Pairwise corridor negotiation vs. fleet scheduling** | Pairwise negotiation is a local, 2–3 robot, often ad hoc decision about who yields. Fleet scheduling is global planning or scheduling of many robots, for example MAPF or AGV traffic managers. | The canonical scenario is local; its 3+ robot and T-junction variants move towards fleet scheduling. |
| **Guarantees vs. empirical performance** | Proofs of safety, liveness or deadlock-freedom under stated assumptions, versus measured success rates and delays. | Rulebooks and bidding are easy to prove safe given a central lock, but liveness under packet loss needs care [@mannucci2019provably]. |
| **Centralised vs. decentralised** | One planner or authority (Open-RMF, VDA 5050 master control, MAPF oracle) versus robots that decide among themselves. | The centralised oracle gives an upper bound on solution quality; the decentralised policies are the object of study. |
| **Human-robot vs. robot-robot right-of-way** | Social navigation optimises comfort and legibility for people; robot-robot right-of-way can rely on explicit messages, shared rules and truthful (or strategic) cost reporting. | Human-robot corridor studies [@pacchierotti2006evaluation; @goahead2015] supply passing-distance and signalling norms, but robots can negotiate explicitly, which humans in corridors cannot. |

## 3. Taxonomy and historical progression

### 3.1 Taxonomy

| Family | Decision locus | Communication | Typical guarantee | Representative works |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Reactive collision avoidance | each robot | none / state broadcast | safety (often), no liveness | VO/RVO/ORCA [@fiorini1998motion; @vandenberg2008rvo; @vandenberg2011orca], BVC [@zhou2017fast], CBF [@wang2017safety], CADRL [@chen2017cadrl] |
| B. Traffic rules and fixed priority | shared rule set | optional | safety; liveness under assumptions | [@kato1992coordinating; @asama1991collision; @pallottino2007decentralized; @bennewitz2002finding; @vandenberg2005prioritized] |
| C. Zone control / resource reservation | central or distributed lock manager | required | deadlock avoidance (Petri nets, graphs) | [@fanti2002event; @reveliotis2000conflict; @yeh1998deadlock; @geozone2023; @hiertraffic2023] |
| D. Centralised planning / MAPF | central solver | required | optimal / bounded-suboptimal / complete | [@sharon2015cbs; @barer2014suboptimal; @okumura2022pibt; @okumura2023lacam; @li2021lifelong] |
| E. Robust plan execution | central supervisor | required, may be lossy | safety + liveness under delay/loss | [@cap2016provably; @honig2019persistent; @pecora2018loosely; @mannucci2019provably; @mannucci2021provably] |
| F. Explicit negotiation, auctions, mechanisms | agents (± auctioneer) | required | efficiency / incentive compatibility | [@smith1980contract; @gerkey2002sold; @dias2006market; @gautier2022negotiated; @azarm1997conflict; @purwin2008theory] |
| G. Game-theoretic navigation | each agent (equilibrium) | none or intent sharing | liveness under game assumptions | [@chandra2025deadlock; @fisac2019hierarchical; @wang2021game; @schwarting2019social] |
| H. Learning (MARL, learned communication) | learned policies | none / learned | empirical | [@sartoretti2019primal; @long2018towards; @bettini2023heterogeneous; @park2023hallucination] |
| I. LLM-mediated negotiation | language agents (± low-level controller) | natural language | empirical | [@mandi2024roco; @garg2024llm; @gamechat2025; @marlin2024] |

### 3.2 Historical progression

- **1987–2001: traffic rules and decoupled priorities.** Early mobile-robot work borrowed road-traffic ideas such as traffic rules, communication of intent and right-of-way. Examples are Grossman's AGV traffic control [@grossman1988traffic], rule-plus-communication collision avoidance [@asama1991collision] and robot traffic rules [@kato1992coordinating]. Others built distributed traffic regulation [@wang1995distributed], decentralised negotiation of motion priorities [@azarm1997conflict], the "cocktail party" decentralised planner [@lumelsky1997decentralized] and plan merging in the MARTHA project [@alami1998multi]. Jäger and Nebel exchanged planned trajectories on proximity, inserted idle times, explicitly *detected* deadlocks and asked planners to replan in turn [@jager2001decentralized]. That is the basic template for "detect, then negotiate". Prioritised and decoupled planning was formalised in [@lavalle1998optimal; @bennewitz2002finding; @vandenberg2005prioritized].
- **1998–2010: AGV zone control and market-based robotics.** Manufacturing research treated guide-path segments as shared resources and avoided deadlock with Petri nets and graph-theoretic tests [@yeh1998deadlock; @reveliotis2000conflict; @fanti2002event; @cyclic2003], reviewed in [@vis2006survey; @leanh2006review]. In parallel, multi-robot task allocation adopted Contract Net and auctions [@smith1980contract; @gerkey2002sold; @gerkey2004formal; @dias2006market; @choi2009consensus]. Large warehouse fleets [@wurman2008coordinating] turned coordination into an industrial problem.
- **2008–2018: reciprocal collision avoidance and formal fleet coordination.** RVO, ORCA and HRVO [@vandenberg2008rvo; @vandenberg2011orca; @snape2011hybrid; @alonsomora2013optimal], BVC [@zhou2017fast] and barrier certificates [@wang2017safety] made decentralised *safety* cheap but left *liveness* open. Prioritised planning gained completeness results for "well-formed infrastructures" [@cap2015prioritized; @cap2015complete], and execution-time guarantees appeared [@cap2016provably; @gregoire2018locally; @pecora2018loosely].
- **2015–present: MAPF at scale.** CBS, ECBS and EECBS [@sharon2015cbs; @barer2014suboptimal; @li2021eecbs], priority-based search [@ma2019searching], PIBT and LaCAM [@okumura2022pibt; @okumura2023lacam], lifelong and windowed MAPF [@ma2017lifelong; @li2021lifelong] and standard benchmarks [@stern2019mapf] made the centralised oracle practical. Robust and kinodynamic execution followed [@honig2016mapf; @honig2019persistent; @atzmon2020robust; @kottinger2022conflict; @dbecbs2025].
- **2016–present: deep RL and learned communication.** CADRL and GA3C-CADRL [@chen2017cadrl; @everett2018motion], end-to-end decentralised policies [@long2018towards; @fan2020distributed], learned MAPF [@sartoretti2019primal; @damani2021primal2; @li2020graph], and learned communication [@foerster2016learning; @sukhbaatar2016learning; @das2019tarmac].
- **2022–present: "social mini-games" and deadlock theory.** Formal characterisation of CBF deadlock [@grover2023before], game-theoretic liveness in doorways and hallways [@chandra2025deadlock], and a 2025–26 taxonomy of the area [@chandra2026social].
- **2023–present: LLM agents.** Dialogue-based multi-robot planning [@mandi2024roco; @kannan2024smart; @chen2024scalable], foundation models as deadlock resolvers [@garg2024llm], LLM-negotiated priority in doorways [@gamechat2025] and LLM negotiation guiding MARL on TurtleBot3s [@marlin2024].

## 4. Per-theme synthesis

### 4.1 Deadlock and livelock in narrow passages

**Why reactive controllers deadlock.** Grover, Liu and Sycara study the closed-loop dynamics of multi-robot CBF quadratic-program controllers [@grover2023before]. They show that geometric *symmetry* of initial conditions and goals necessarily leads to deadlock, that heterogeneous controller parameters can help, and they use duality theory to characterise configurations "during" deadlock and design controllers to escape it "after". Their earlier conference version reports experiments on Khepera-IV robots [@grover2020deadlock]. The head-on corridor is the extreme symmetric case: without asymmetry injected by rules, priorities, perturbations [@pseudogoal2024] or communication, a CBF or ORCA controller has no reason to give way. Zheng et al. reach a similar conclusion for ORCA-like QPs on nonholonomic robots. They add "masked velocities" (MCCA), a lightweight form of cooperation that propagates through the team, and handle heading oscillation for differential-drive robots, which is relevant to TurtleBot3 [@zheng2023deadlock]. Alonso-Mora et al. combine reactive mission and motion planning with explicit deadlock resolution [@reactive2017deadlock], and DS-MPEPC changes the trajectory cost to reduce deadlocking behaviour [@arul2023dsmpepc].

**Detect-then-coordinate.** Jäger and Nebel detect deadlock and trigger sequential replanning [@jager2001decentralized]. Dergachev and Yakovlev run ORCA by default and, once a deadlock is detected, form a *locally confined MAPF* instance among the robots involved, solved with Push-and-Rotate or ECBS. In tight passages this raised success rates from roughly 10–15% to 95–99% in their experiments [@dergachev2021distributed]. This hybrid is an important baseline because it mirrors the planned protocol: robots drive reactively until a conflict is detected, then switch to an explicit decision procedure. Zhou et al. give a distributed stop-and-resume scheme on labelled transition systems for robots on fixed closed paths [@zhou2017collision]. DLSC uses mode-based subgoals to escape deadlocks in maze-like environments [@park2023dlsc].

**Delays, disturbances and communication faults.** Čáp, Gregoire and Frazzoli prove that a simple execution rule, which respects the planned precedence of robots at shared regions, is safe *and* deadlock-free even when robots are delayed by disturbances such as passing humans. Naive liveness-preserving rules scale poorly with disturbance intensity [@cap2016provably; @gregoire2018locally]. Mannucci et al. drop the perfect-communication assumption from a centralised loosely coupled coordinator. With delays only, safety is guaranteed. With packet loss, the probability of unsafety is bounded as a function of the channel model [@mannucci2019provably]. They later add liveness with online goal posting and heterogeneous robots, "without hand-coded rules [or] fixed robot priorities" [@mannucci2021provably]. Matos et al. present a TEA*-based AGV system that tolerates zones with no network coverage [@commfail2021]. Together these works make **latency and packet loss** a first-class variable, and show that it must be modelled explicitly instead of hoping the protocol is robust. Communication in multi-robot systems more broadly is reviewed in [@gielis2022critical].

### 4.2 Right-of-way, AGV traffic rules and industrial frameworks

**Zone control.** Classical AGV systems split the guide path into zones that one vehicle at a time may hold. Deadlocks are predicted and avoided by checking for cyclic waits [@yeh1998deadlock; @reveliotis2000conflict; @fanti2002event; @cyclic2003]. Lee et al. note that this work assumes "sufficient space between nodes and links". They partition zones using vehicle geometry and guide-path topology, which matters for physically wide robots in narrow aisles [@geozone2023]. Pratissoli et al. describe a three-layer (topological, path, roadmap) traffic manager with a deadlock-prevention coordination step for real industrial layouts [@hiertraffic2023]. Earlier work from the same group covers ensemble and decentralised AGV coordination [@digani2015ensemble; @draganjac2016decentralized; @decagv2017]. A one-lane corridor with bays maps directly onto zone control: each corridor segment and each bay is a resource. The bays work like the "storage" or "parking" nodes used in AGV deadlock-avoidance proofs and in MAPD "standby" locations [@yamauchi2022standby]. Reviews of AMR intralogistics planning and control [@fragapane2021planning] and of AGV design and control [@vis2006survey; @leanh2006review] give the industrial context.

**Industrial frameworks and standards.**

- *Open-RMF* is the ROS 2 ecosystem's multi-fleet coordination framework [@openrmf; @ros2multirobotbook]. Its `rmf_traffic` package "implements the algorithms and data structures that are used for scheduling and negotiating mobile robot traffic between multiple agents" [@rmftraffic]. Fleet adapters publish intended itineraries to a shared schedule database. When conflicts are detected, the fleets involved negotiate by exchanging alternative proposals. This is the closest deployed analogue of the planned "rule-based" and "bidding" protocols, and the **natural industrial comparator** to discuss (and possibly integrate) in the ROS 2 Jazzy stack.
- *VDA 5050* standardises the interface between a central master control and AGVs (orders as node/edge graphs, state messages) [@vda5050]. Traffic management and conflict resolution stay with the master control, so under VDA 5050 the pull-in-bay decision is centralised. This is the "centralised oracle" family.
- *MassRobotics AMR Interoperability Standard* specifies status and location reporting so that robots from different vendors can share a facility [@massrobotics]. It supports *awareness*, but contains no negotiation protocol.
- *ISO 3691-4:2023* covers safety requirements and verification for driverless industrial trucks and their systems [@iso36914]. Its contents were not consulted for this review (paywalled). It should still be checked for the safety-related requirements on clearances and speed that the min-clearance metric and the reversing manoeuvre will have to respect in any real deployment.

### 4.3 MAPF and priority-based planning as the centralised baseline

MAPF offers the strongest *centralised oracle*. Variants and benchmark maps are standardised in [@stern2019mapf], and open problems are surveyed in [@salzman2020research]. CBS is optimal [@sharon2015cbs]; ECBS and EECBS are bounded-suboptimal [@barer2014suboptimal; @li2021eecbs]; symmetry-breaking reasoning removes the very corridor-swap conflicts that slow CBS down [@li2020new; @li2021pairwise]. Prioritised planning is fast but incomplete in general [@vandenberg2005prioritized; @cap2015prioritized; @ma2019searching; @wu2020multi]. PIBT adds **priority inheritance with backtracking**: a high-priority agent that is blocked passes its priority to the blocker, which must move, and backtracking guarantees reachability in biconnected graphs [@okumura2022pibt]. LaCAM builds a complete search on top of PIBT-style successor generation [@okumura2023lacam]. Lifelong and windowed MAPF (RHCR) resolves conflicts only within a rolling horizon [@li2021lifelong; @ma2017lifelong]. Earlier cooperative pathfinding [@silver2005cooperative], optimal graph-based multirobot planning [@yu2016optimal] and continuous-time optimal planning [@cohen2019optimal] complete the picture. MAPF can also be posed as a combinatorial auction [@amir2015mapfauction], a bridge to Section 4.5.

**Narrow corridors.** Moan et al. target width-1 corridors ("doorways") in houses and offices. Their conflict-resolution strategies cut waiting steps per robot by up to 94% compared with earlier experience-based methods [@experience2024narrow]. Singh et al. schedule nodes to robots for lifelong operation in long narrow corridors, with an explicit deadlock-detection-and-resolution step [@nodetorobot2023]. Pertzovsky et al.'s MACGA has agents build "corridors" towards their goals and *evacuate* other agents out of them, an explicit move-aside primitive analogous to pulling into a bay [@macga2025]. McBeth et al. use topological guidance for congested environments with many narrow passages [@topoguide2023].

**Kinodynamics and reversing.** Grid MAPF ignores orientation, acceleration and reversing cost. MAPF-POST post-processes plans for kinematic limits [@honig2016mapf], persistent execution uses an action-dependency graph to tolerate slow-downs [@honig2019persistent], and delay-aware variants exist [@ma2017delay; @atzmon2020robust]. Kinodynamic CBS [@kottinger2022conflict], CL-MAPF for car-like robots [@wen2022clmapf] and the discontinuity-bounded db-ECBS and db-LaCAM [@dbecbs2025; @dblacam2026] plan directly in continuous state spaces. For TurtleBot3 (differential drive, symmetric forward and reverse kinematics, but rear-facing sensing limited to the 360° LDS) the main gap is that **reversing is rarely priced differently from driving forward**. An oracle for this study should charge extra for reverse motion and turning.

### 4.4 Decentralised collision avoidance and its failure in corridors

Velocity obstacles [@fiorini1998motion], RVO [@vandenberg2008rvo], ORCA [@vandenberg2011orca], HRVO, which targets the reciprocal "dance" [@snape2011hybrid], and their nonholonomic and kinodynamic extensions [@alonsomora2013optimal; @alonsomora2018cooperative] all share the same structure. Each agent solves a local optimisation that is symmetric in the agents. In a corridor that is too narrow to pass, the feasible velocity set collapses to "stop", which is a stable deadlock [@dergachev2021distributed; @zheng2023deadlock]. BVC planning [@zhou2017fast] and barrier certificates [@wang2017safety] guarantee collision avoidance, but CBF-based liveness requires additional mechanisms [@grover2023before]. The dynamic window approach [@fox1997dwa], which underlies common ROS local planners [@macenski2020marathon], behaves the same way. Learned reactive policies (CADRL and GA3C-CADRL [@chen2017cadrl; @everett2018motion; @everett2021collision], end-to-end sensor-level policies [@long2018towards; @fan2020distributed]) can learn conventions such as passing on the right [@chen2017socially]. They are trained on open-space crossings, though, and do not reason about reversing into a refuge. Park et al. say directly that standard navigation stacks, when two robots meet in a narrow hallway, "may either turn around to find an alternative route or collide". Their PHHP method learns where to *hallucinate* virtual obstacles so that unmodified planners pass smoothly, tested on physical robots without explicit communication [@park2023hallucination]. Pedestrian-inspired and social-force approaches [@knepper2012pedestrian; @helbing1995social] supply passing heuristics but offer no liveness guarantee in one-lane spaces.

### 4.5 Explicit negotiation protocols, games and norms

**Protocols.** Contract Net [@smith1980contract] and auction-based coordination [@gerkey2002sold; @dias2006market; @choi2009consensus] were built for *task* allocation, but they carry over to *right-of-way* allocation, where the resource being auctioned is the corridor. Azarm and Schmidt negotiate motion priorities among robots in local conflicts using online motion planning [@azarm1997conflict], and Purwin et al. negotiate reserved regions among decentralised agents [@purwin2008theory]. Automated negotiation [@jennings2001automated] and argumentation-based negotiation [@rahwan2003argumentation] provide richer protocols, where agents exchange reasons such as "I carry an urgent payload" or "my refuge is 0.5 m behind me". These map onto the ordered YAML rulebook. Plan merging [@alami1998multi] is an early distributed-negotiation architecture for robot fleets.

**Games and mechanism design.** Two robots competing for a single-lane corridor play a *Chicken*/Hawk–Dove game [@maynardsmith1973logic]: mutual "hawk" (both advance) leads to deadlock or collision, and each prefers the other to yield. With divisible delay costs this is a bargaining problem [@nash1950bargaining]. In alternating-offers bargaining a discount factor, which here means the cost of waiting, determines the split [@rubinstein1982perfect]. **Cost-based bidding** ("lower bid yields") is a sealed-bid mechanism. If each robot's bid is its private cost of yielding, truthful bidding is not automatically incentive-compatible: each robot gains by over-reporting its cost. Second-price/VCG-style payments [@vickrey1961counterspeculation; @nisan2001algorithmic] or credit ledgers are the textbook fix. Gautier et al. design exactly such an auction for *non-cooperative* MAPF, so that rational, self-interested robots from different owners reach goals while system cost is minimised [@gautier2022negotiated]. Intersection-management auctions [@carlino2013auction; @vasirani2012market] and game-theoretic intersection control [@elhenawy2015intersection; @gtim2024] are close road analogues. For fleets from the same owner, truthfulness is assumed by design; for **heterogeneous or multi-vendor fleets** (the MassRobotics use case [@massrobotics]) it is not.

**Social conventions.** Social laws such as "keep right" or "the robot nearer to a refuge yields" can be designed offline so that conflicts never arise [@shoham1995social]. They are also learned implicitly by RL [@chen2017socially] and are part of human driving codes [@highwaycode]. The ordered rulebook (urgency > first-in-corridor > closest refuge behind > lower ID) is a lexicographic social law. Its safety follows from the central lock, and its *liveness* depends on the rules producing a strict total order and on both robots seeing the same facts, which latency or packet loss can break.

### 4.6 Game-theoretic "social mini-games"

Chandra et al. define *social mini-games*: doorways, narrow hallways and corridor intersections where agents compete for space. They pose these as non-cooperative, non-communicative, partially observable receding-horizon games. A minimally invasive perturbation, based on discrete-time CBFs, pushes the joint state into "liveness sets" [@chandra2025deadlock]. They test on F1/10 cars, a Clearpath Jackal and a Boston Dynamics Spot in doorway, hallway and intersection scenarios. Metrics are success rate, collision rate, stop time, change in velocity, path deviation, time-to-goal and flow rate. A follow-up survey and taxonomy reviews SMG solvers across MAPF, MARL, game-theoretic and optimisation communities, and notes that publications "make different assumptions" that prevent comparison [@chandra2026social]. Related work includes bilevel non-cooperative social navigation [@chandra2023decentralized], opinion-dynamics symmetry breaking [@alghamdi2025opinion], LiveNet [@livenet2024] and pseudo-goal perturbation [@pseudogoal2024]. Game-theoretic planners from driving, including iterative LQ games [@fridovichkeil2020efficient], ALGAMES [@lecleach2022algames], hierarchical games [@fisac2019hierarchical], multi-vehicle competitive planning [@wang2021game] and social value orientation [@schwarting2019social], provide solvers for richer asymmetric-cost versions.

### 4.7 Learning approaches

MARL baselines suitable for the study include MADDPG [@lowe2017maddpg], QMIX [@rashid2018qmix] and MAPPO [@yu2022mappo], with sequential social dilemmas [@leibo2017multi] as a lens on yielding as cooperation. Learned communication comes in several forms: DIAL and RIAL [@foerster2016learning], CommNet [@sukhbaatar2016learning], TarMAC [@das2019tarmac], GNN-based MAPF [@li2020graph; @li2021message] and the emergence of self-interested or adversarial communication [@blumenkamp2020emergence]. Real-robot deployment of decentralised GNN policies is shown in [@blumenkamp2022framework]. VMAS provides vectorised 2-D multi-robot environments [@bettini2022vmas], including `give_way`: two agents in a narrow corridor must swap places, and an asymmetric opening in the middle fits only one agent, so "the optimal policy is for one agent to give way to the other" [@vmasgiveway]. the scenario documentation notes that it "requires heterogeneous behaviour", which is the motivation for heterogeneous MARL such as HetGPPO [@bettini2023heterogeneous], and BenchMARL standardises training and evaluation [@bettini2024benchmarl]. PRIMAL and PRIMAL2 learn decentralised MAPF policies, PRIMAL2 for lifelong and structured, corridor-rich maps [@sartoretti2019primal; @damani2021primal2]. Learned approaches remain empirical; none of these works gives liveness guarantees, and sim-to-real transfer of a give-way policy to a ROS 2 robot is rarely shown. MARLIN is the exception (Section 4.8).

### 4.8 LLM-based multi-robot negotiation and coordination (2023 onward)

- **RoCo** uses one LLM agent per robot arm. The agents talk to each other to agree on sub-tasks and waypoints, and environment feedback (for example collision checks) is fed back in-context. RoCoBench has 6 tasks, and GPT-4 reaches 86.7% success on a path-planning task within 5 attempts [@mandi2024roco]. The setting is manipulation, not navigation, but the dialogue-and-feedback loop is directly reusable.
- **Task-level planners**: SMART-LLM [@kannan2024smart], centralised vs. decentralised LLM planning architectures [@chen2024scalable], COHERENT for heterogeneous teams [@liu2024coherent], cooperative embodied agents [@zhang2024building] and a 2025–26 survey of LLMs for multi-robot systems [@li2025llmmrs]. A general survey of LLM-based multi-agent systems is [@guo2024llmmulti].
- **Garg et al.** use LLMs and VLMs as *high-level deadlock resolvers* above a learned low-level controller. When robots stall, the foundation model picks a leader and waypoints. Tests cover 5–50 agents in "Room" and "Maze" environments with GPT-4/4o and Claude-3 models; metrics are reach rate, number of interventions, time, tokens and distance travelled [@garg2024llm].
- **GameChat** has agents in symmetric doorways or intersections *talk* in natural language (gpt-4o-mini) to establish priority, for example "an emergency that threatens many lives takes precedence", and then run a game-theoretic controller so that the higher-priority agent passes first [@gamechat2025]. This is an LLM version of the "urgency" rule.
- **MARLIN** lets robots negotiate actions through an LLM (Llama 3.1 8B) early in MARL training, then hands over to the learned policy. The scenarios are corridors with *one or two side slots*: "Asymmetrical Two Slot Corridor", "Single Slot Corridor", "Symmetrical Two Slot Corridor" and "Maze-Like Corridor". These were validated on **two TurtleBot3 Waffle robots running ROS 2 Humble** [@marlin2024]. It is the closest prior physical setup to the present study. However, it uses grid-like moves, evaluates against MARL and LLM-only baselines rather than rules, bidding or an oracle, and does not vary latency, urgency or fairness.
- **Negotiation in language** in general: Deal-or-No-Deal [@lewis2017deal], Diplomacy with Cicero [@meta2022diplomacy] and LLM-stakeholder negotiation games [@abdelnabi2024cooperation] show that LLMs can bargain, deceive and be manipulated. That last point matters if LLM negotiation is used between robots from different owners.

### 4.9 Social navigation benchmarks and evaluation protocols

Francis et al. give consensus principles and guidelines for evaluating social navigation: metrics, scenarios, benchmarks, datasets and simulators [@francis2025principles]. Other contributions include SocNavBench [@biswas2022socnavbench], SocialGym and SocialGym 2.0 (the latter a simulator for multi-robot learning and navigation) [@holtz2022socialgym; @socialgym2aaai], SEAN 2.0 [@tsoi2022sean2], Arena-Rosnav and Arena-Bench, which build on Flatland [@kastner2021arena; @kastner2022arenabench], BARN [@perille2020benchmarking; @xiao2022barn], and scenario complexity characterisation [@stratton2024complexity]. Surveys: [@kruse2013human; @mavrogiannis2023core; @mirsky2024conflict; @gao2022evaluation]. Mirsky et al.'s conflict-avoidance survey is the most relevant, because it organises the literature around the very corridor and doorway conflicts this study targets [@mirsky2024conflict]. Human-robot corridor-passing studies give useful priors for *robot-robot* behaviour: preferred passing distance and early lateral shifts [@pacchierotti2005human; @pacchierotti2006evaluation], resolving a narrow-passage deadlock with a person by waiting in a non-disturbing position or queueing [@goahead2015], and making intent legible with light or gaze signals [@fernandez2018passive; @hart2020using]. Pérez-D'Arpino et al. train RL navigation in constrained pedestrian environments, including doorways [@perezdarpino2021robot]. These benchmarks are *human-robot*; none ships a robot-robot head-on corridor-with-bays scenario with negotiation-specific metrics such as reverse distance or messages exchanged.

### 4.10 Road-traffic analogues

The UK Highway Code's single-track-road rule describes the canonical scenario directly: "pull into a passing place on your left, or wait opposite a passing place to let the other vehicle pass" [@highwaycode]. Youssef et al. survey 243 UK drivers on narrow-passage give-way decisions and on how they perceive communication cues [@narrowpassageuk2023]. Li et al. propose AV-to-human-driver negotiation via in-vehicle displays on bottleneck roads [@avmvbottleneck2023]. Baumann et al. design a V2V cooperative bottleneck protocol (e.g. at a roadblock). Its main challenge in dense traffic is *acceptance and fairness*: limiting the waiting imposed on any one user by alternating priority by queue position [@bottleneckiv2023]. That supports including **fairness** as a primary metric. Decentralised cooperative planning with MCTS [@kurzer2018decentralized; @kurzer2018continuous], cooperative behaviour coordination [@kessler2019cooperative], courteous planning [@sun2018courteous], effects on human actions [@sadigh2016planning] and right-of-way formalisms such as RSS [@shalevshwartz2017formal] round out the driving view. So do intersection-management protocols, both reservation-based [@dresner2008multiagent] and delay- and packet-loss-tolerant [@delaytolerant2019], and surveys of CAV coordination [@hult2016coordination; @riostorres2017survey; @hafner2022survey].

## 6. Research gaps and opportunities: positioning the pull-in-bay study

**Gap 1 — No common benchmark or metric set for robot-robot head-on negotiation with refuges.** Corridor and doorway conflicts appear in MAPF grids [@experience2024narrow; @macga2025], social mini-games [@chandra2025deadlock], MARL toy scenarios [@vmasgiveway] and MARLIN's slotted corridors [@marlin2024], but every paper builds its own scene and metrics. Surveys explicitly ask for comparable assumptions and benchmarks [@chandra2026social; @francis2025principles; @stratton2024complexity]. *Opportunity:* release a parameterised Gazebo Harmonic + ROS 2 Jazzy scenario suite: bay side and position, number of bays, bay depth versus robot footprint, T-junctions, doorways, robot count, and heterogeneous footprints, speeds and payloads. Pair it with a fixed metric set (Section 8) and a seeded protocol.

**Gap 2 — No head-to-head comparison of decision mechanisms under the same physics and communication model.** Each family is usually compared only against reactive baselines or its own ablations. Rules [@kato1992coordinating; @shoham1995social], auctions and bidding [@gerkey2002sold; @gautier2022negotiated], centralised MAPF or zone control [@okumura2022pibt; @hiertraffic2023], industrial negotiation (`rmf_traffic`) [@rmftraffic], LLM dialogue [@gamechat2025; @garg2024llm] and MARL [@bettini2023heterogeneous] have never been evaluated side by side. Latency and packet loss are modelled in centralised coordinators [@mannucci2019provably; @commfail2021; @delaytolerant2019] but not in pairwise negotiation protocols. *Opportunity:* the five planned policies, with a common message layer that injects delay and loss, give the first controlled comparison. The **price of decentralisation** (oracle vs. negotiation) and the **price of communication impairment** can then be reported as ratios.

**Gap 3 — Refuge selection with reversing cost, asymmetric private costs, and incentives and fairness over repeated encounters.** MAPF and AGV work usually treats a bay as just another node and charges reversing the same as forward motion; kinodynamic MAPF is recent [@kottinger2022conflict; @dbecbs2025]. Game-theoretic SMG work resolves *who goes first* by slowing down, not by *who backs into which bay* [@chandra2025deadlock]. Bidding protocols assume truthful costs, while mechanism design shows that self-interested robots may misreport [@vickrey1961counterspeculation; @nisan2001algorithmic; @gautier2022negotiated]. Fairness across repeated encounters is raised in road bottlenecks [@bottleneckiv2023] but rarely measured for robots. *Opportunity:* model cost-to-yield explicitly (reverse distance, turning, payload, urgency, battery). Compare truthful versus strategic bidding, with and without VCG-style payments or credit ledgers, and report fairness across encounters with Jain's index [@jain1984fairness].

**Secondary opportunities.** (i) Heterogeneous fleets: different footprints and speeds, and a multi-vendor interoperability story via MassRobotics or VDA 5050 [@massrobotics; @vda5050]. (ii) LLM negotiation as a policy, measured on latency, tokens and robustness to adversarial or "selfish" prompts [@abdelnabi2024cooperation]. (iii) Formal liveness of the rulebook under message loss, in the style of [@cap2016provably; @mannucci2019provably]. (iv) Sim-to-real on TurtleBot3, following the MARLIN hardware precedent [@marlin2024].

## 8. Recommended metrics

| Metric | Definition | Literature support |
|---|---|---|
| Success rate / deadlock rate / livelock rate | episode reaches all goals within timeout; deadlock = no progress for *T* s with zero velocity; livelock = no progress with oscillating motion | [@chandra2025deadlock; @dergachev2021distributed; @grover2023before; @garg2024llm] |
| Time-to-resolve | from conflict detection to the first robot exiting the shared segment | analogous to stop time in [@chandra2025deadlock] |
| Makespan, sum-of-costs / total delay | max and sum of arrival times; delay versus a free-flow single-robot run | [@stern2019mapf; @macga2025] |
| Reverse distance and reverse time | path length driven in reverse; extra turning | new; motivated by kinodynamic MAPF [@kottinger2022conflict; @wen2022clmapf] |
| Collisions and minimum clearance | contacts; minimum footprint-to-footprint distance | [@chandra2025deadlock; @francis2025principles]; clearance norms [@pacchierotti2006evaluation; @iso36914] |
| Path deviation, Δv / smoothness | deviation from nominal path; velocity changes | [@chandra2025deadlock] |
| Messages / bytes exchanged; decision latency | protocol overhead; time from first message to agreement | [@gielis2022critical; @garg2024llm] (tokens) |
| Robustness to latency and loss | metric degradation versus delay and loss rate | [@mannucci2019provably; @delaytolerant2019; @commfail2021] |
| Fairness | Jain's index over per-robot delay across repeated encounters; yield share | [@jain1984fairness; @bottleneckiv2023] |
| Throughput / flow rate (3+ robots) | robots per minute through the corridor | [@chandra2025deadlock; @li2021lifelong] |
| Price of decentralisation | metric(policy) / metric(oracle) | standard in MAPF/coordination comparisons [@cap2015prioritized] |

Report means with 95% confidence intervals over seeded trials and paired comparisons across policies on identical seeds, following the evaluation guidance in [@francis2025principles].
